When Ideologies Think for Us

A reflection on power, identity, and freedom of conscience.

We live in an age that prides itself on unprecedented intellectual freedom. Never have we had so much access to knowledge, so many opportunities to compare viewpoints, or so many tools for challenging traditional structures of power.

And yet it is striking how often we still need someone to tell us what to think, which causes to defend, and who our adversaries are.

Reading the La Razón article “Woke 2.0: El virus muta para sobrevivir” led me to reflect on a question much deeper than the controversy surrounding any particular ideological movement.

Are we truly free when we think, or have we simply learned to treat other people's ideas as our own?

This is not about questioning the legitimacy of social demands or denying inequalities that need correcting. Nor is it about dismissing the political convictions of people who think differently.

It is about understanding how ideas—even those born of legitimate aspirations—can become structures of power that shape our perception of reality.

The deepest power does not need to impose itself

We usually associate power with governments, institutions, large corporations, or economic resources. Yet there is a subtler form of power: the ability to determine how we interpret the world.

Those who establish what is morally acceptable, what counts as a threat, and which opinions deserve recognition wield extraordinary influence.

Michel Foucault explored the relationship between power and the production of knowledge. Antonio Gramsci examined how certain worldviews become hegemonic until they appear natural. Both perspectives suggest that power does not always need to prohibit. Sometimes it merely needs certain questions to stop being asked.

Once we fully internalize an ideological framework, we may begin policing our own opinions—not necessarily because anyone forces us to, but because challenging dominant beliefs within our circle risks losing approval, belonging, or moral legitimacy.

This dynamic belongs to no single political tradition. It can appear in religious organizations, corporations, universities, social movements, and online communities. Even those who oppose dominant thinking may eventually create orthodoxies of their own.

Groupthink is defined not by the content of its ideas, but by the difficulty of questioning them without consequences.

Here lies an unsettling paradox: a society can multiply its declarations of freedom while shrinking the spaces in which dissent remains possible.

We need a “them” to build an “us”

Human beings need to belong. Family, community, nation, religion, or a shared cause can give us identity and meaning. There is nothing inherently wrong with that; belonging is essential to our social nature.

Problems begin when our identity constantly requires an adversary. Someone who thinks differently stops being a person whose arguments we can debate and becomes someone whose moral legitimacy we question.

We no longer debate ideas. We classify people. Human complexity is reduced to seemingly simple categories: progressive and conservative, enlightened and ignorant, victim and perpetrator, good and bad.

Reality rarely fits those divisions. Someone may champion a just cause while being deeply mistaken about their methods. Someone else may criticize particular social movements without rejecting the rights they advocate.

Confusing those two dimensions impoverishes our understanding and turns public debate into a competition for moral superiority.

When we need to dehumanize an opponent to justify our beliefs, we have probably begun to lose our own intellectual freedom.

Ideologies change—but does our relationship with power?

The article argues that some movements are adapting their language to new social and political circumstances. This interpretation deserves scrutiny rather than automatic acceptance.

Ideologies evolve for different reasons. Sometimes they acknowledge mistakes; sometimes society uncovers new needs; and sometimes familiar strategies cease to work. These explanations can coexist.

One especially sensitive example today is solidarity with the Palestinian population. Civilian suffering, the protection of people's rights, and the hope for a just peace deserve consideration in their own right. At the same time, flags and humanitarian causes can become symbols of collective identity and political mobilization. Recognizing that possibility neither makes solidarity opportunistic nor means everyone who demonstrates has the same motives.

Out of respect for those affected, we should distinguish humanitarian commitment from using tragedy to sort others into moral camps. The dignity of victims must not depend on how useful their stories are to any movement.

This is why I find it inadequate to judge ideological change merely by its slogans. What happens behind the words? Are freedoms genuinely expanded? Are inequalities reduced? Is disagreement permitted? Are mistakes acknowledged? Are principles applied regardless of who benefits?

These questions offer firmer criteria for evaluating any movement than its political identity. An ideology can change its vocabulary while leaving its relationship with power untouched. It can also retain its fundamental values while learning to correct its contradictions.

Real transformation is not about replacing one slogan with another. It is about changing our relationship with truth, freedom, and other people.

The outrage industry

There is another dimension I consider crucial. Contemporary ideologies compete not only for control of institutions but also for our attention—and attention has become one of the most valuable economic resources of our age.

Digital platforms have multiplied access to knowledge, yet they have also created environments in which outrage, fear, and confrontation can achieve extraordinary visibility. Emotionally charged messages can travel faster than rigorous, nuanced explanations.

This does not mean every complaint is exaggerated or every protest manipulated. It means we must distinguish the real importance of an event from the emotional intensity with which it is presented.

We can spend hours debating distant conflicts while overlooking problems in our own communities. We can feel deeply morally committed without taking a single action that changes reality. We can even confuse digital activity with meaningful contributions to collective well-being.

So, whenever information provokes a powerful reaction, I think we should ask an additional question:

Why is this issue demanding my attention right now—and who benefits from the way it is being presented to me?

This is not about suspecting everything. It is about preserving our ability to choose what deserves our time, energy, and emotional investment.

The paradox of defending freedom while demanding obedience

One of the deepest contradictions of ideological movements arises when their stated aims clash with their methods.

We can celebrate diversity while showing intolerance towards particular opinions. We can champion individual liberty while trying to impose a single way of life. We can denounce exclusion and create new forms of it. We can defend free expression while systematically discrediting those who advocate social rights.

No political tradition is entirely free of these contradictions. That is why I believe ethical consistency is tested precisely when our principles require us to recognize the rights of people who disagree with us.

Defending the freedom of those who think like us is relatively easy. The real test comes when we must grant that same freedom to those who challenge our beliefs. This does not mean all opinions are equally true or all conduct acceptable. We must distinguish someone's right to speak from the validity of their arguments.

An ethic that protects only our own risks becoming a strategy of belonging disguised as a universal principle.

Critical thinking begins when we challenge our own certainties

One point seems particularly important to me: identifying other people's biases is far easier than recognizing our own.

We all build mental models to interpret reality. We all select information and carry experiences that shape how we see the world. And we can all assume our conclusions are independently reasoned while attributing opposing views to ideological manipulation.

That asymmetry is profoundly human. Critical thinking therefore cannot stop at dismantling our opponents' narratives. It must also examine the ideas that give us security, recognition, and moral satisfaction.

There is something intellectually liberating about admitting that someone who disagrees with us may have noticed a truth we missed. Listening does not mean agreeing. Understanding does not mean excusing. Changing our minds should not be seen as defeat—quite the opposite.

The ability to revise our convictions in the face of better arguments and evidence is among the highest expressions of intellectual autonomy.

Perhaps true maturity means learning to live with uncertainty, accepting that we can be wrong, and recognizing that the world's complexity exceeds any ideology claiming to explain it completely.

Understanding power without becoming what we criticize

There is a question that especially concerns me. Learning to recognize manipulation also equips us to manipulate. We can see how narratives are built, emotions mobilized, and collective behavior shaped by structures of influence.

But that knowledge brings ethical responsibility. What do we do with it? We can use it to influence people ever more effectively, or to protect our independence and help build freer relationships.

Understanding mechanisms of domination should not lead us to reproduce them. Nor should we confuse insight with cynicism.

The fact that a social cause can be politically exploited does not make the suffering it describes unreal. An organization gaining recognition by defending values does not invalidate those values. An ideology serving functions of power does not mean all its claims are false.

Permanent suspicion can itself become an intellectual prison. Someone who reads every act of solidarity as a hidden strategy risks losing the ability to recognize goodness when it appears.

True insight is not finding hidden interests behind every action, but learning to tell when those interests genuinely explain what we are observing.

The freedom to belong intellectually to no one

Over the years I have increasingly valued curiosity and the willingness to revisit our certainties. Experience does not automatically make us wiser.

We may accumulate knowledge while interpreting the world through the same old prejudices. We may master many theories and still be unable to listen to someone who disagrees. We may passionately defend freedom while handing our judgment over to an organization, movement, or ideology.

Perhaps learning is not merely acquiring new knowledge. It also means letting go of interpretations that no longer help us understand reality.

Reading the article about the so-called Woke 2.0 brought these mechanisms to mind. Yet it would be a mistake to limit the reflection to the movement under criticism. Describing an ideology as a virus is itself a narrative device: it turns a complex political and cultural phenomenon into a threat against which we are expected to take a stand.

That does not automatically invalidate the author's arguments. It simply reminds us to apply the same critical rigor to every discourse, including those we agree with.

We are not intellectually free simply because we have recognized the manipulation of those who think differently. We become free when we recognize the same mechanisms within the ideas we ourselves defend.

Perhaps the great challenge of our time is not to replace one ideology with another but to live with our convictions without turning them into prisons: to defend values while remaining willing to revise them, to take part in collective projects without surrendering our conscience, and to understand power without losing our humanity.

Above all, we must retain something no organization, movement, or social structure should take away:

The ability to think for ourselves, even when doing so means questioning what we have always believed.

For the deepest freedom is not merely that nobody tells us what to think.

It is retaining the ability to ask whether they are right—even when everyone is trying to tell us.
